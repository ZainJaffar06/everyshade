Skin health for every skin tone · New Jersey

Skin cancer doesn't look the same on everyone.

Every Shade is a student group at Rutgers University–New Brunswick. We give free talks to senior centers, libraries, and community groups on how to spot skin cancer on every skin tone, and how to get checked.

Why we started

Most of the pictures doctors learn from show light skin.

On darker skin, a rash can look purple or brown instead of red, and melanoma often shows up in places people don't think to check. When it's missed, it's caught later.

15%

of images in six common medical student study resources showed skin of color.

66% vs 90%

five-year melanoma survival for Black patients compared with white patients, largely because it's found at a later stage.

46%

of melanomas of a known type in Black patients were the kind that grows on the palms, the soles of the feet, or under the nails (2% in white patients).

Sources: Perlman et al., Int J Womens Dermatol, 2021. Culp and Lunsford, Preventing Chronic Disease (CDC), 2019.

What our talk covers

  • What melanoma and other common skin cancers are
  • Things that raise your risk, like sun exposure, a history of sunburns, family history, and age
  • What warning signs look like on light, medium, and dark skin
  • The spots people miss: palms, soles of the feet, between the toes, and under the nails
  • How to do a skin check at home, step by step
  • When to see a doctor, and where to go if you don't have insurance

Co-founder

Zain Jaffar

Co-founder, Every Shade · Honors College, Rutgers University–New Brunswick

Zain is a pre-med student in the Honors College at Rutgers University–New Brunswick, studying Cell Biology and Neuroscience with a minor in Data Science. He works as a dermatology and Mohs surgery medical assistant, does melanoma research at Mass General Brigham and Harvard Medical School, and does teledermatology research with Stanford Dermatology.

He co-founded Every Shade after seeing how often skin problems on darker skin get missed, both in clinic and in the photos that doctors and AI tools learn from.

# DermaSensor got cleared. Adoption is the harder exam.

*Derm × AI, issue 1 · by Zain Jaffar · ready to publish the week of November 16, 2026 (facts checked Nov 10)*

**My take: the first FDA-cleared AI skin cancer device can make a family doctor miss fewer cancers. It has not yet shown anyone will pay for that, and its darker-skin evidence is thinner than the marketing.**

## What it is

DermaSensor is a handheld wand that shines light on a mole, reads how the light scatters back (elastic scattering spectroscopy), and returns "Investigate Further" or "Monitor." The FDA granted it De Novo authorization on January 12, 2024 (DEN230008), the first AI-enabled device for skin cancer evaluation in the US. The indication is narrow: patients 40 and over, lesions a clinician already finds suspicious, used by physicians who are not dermatologists. It is a second opinion for the family doctor, not a screening tool. It sells as a monthly subscription, and in September 2026 the company announced four distribution partnerships.

## Why it matters

Most skin cancers are first seen by someone who is not a dermatologist, and the moment a spot gets reassured instead of referred is where melanomas get lost. DermaSensor targets that moment. In the DERM-SUCCESS trial (22 sites, 1,005 patients, 1,579 lesions), it reached 95.5% sensitivity against biopsy for the three common skin cancers, with a 96.6% negative predictive value. In a reader study of 108 primary care physicians, it moved management sensitivity from 82.0% to 91.4%.

That is the useful kind of number: not "AI beats dermatologists" but "AI makes a non-expert less likely to miss." The cost is in the other column. Specificity for referral fell from 44.2% to 32.4%, so the device makes doctors refer more. In a dermatology system with months-long waits, someone has to carry every extra referral.

## The catch

**Reimbursement is not settled.** There is no dedicated CPT code. The company's guidance points to billing under an office visit or an unlisted code (96999 or 17999), concedes some payers call it investigational, and offers consultants to argue each claim. A step that adds time to a 15-minute visit and may not be paid for tends to stay in the drawer.

**Specificity is low everywhere.** In DERM-ASSESS III (440 biopsied lesions), melanoma sensitivity was 96% and specificity 33%. That is defensible for a rule-out tool, but the value then depends on what the downstream clinic does with the extra referrals.

**The skin-of-color evidence is a subgroup, not a study.** The company says performance was "consistent regardless of pigmentation." Behind that: 72% of DERM-SUCCESS patients were Fitzpatrick I to III. The IV to VI subgroup had 276 patients and 48 cancers, with sensitivity of 92.2% (95% CI 82.1 to 96.8) versus 96.5% in lighter skin. Forty-eight cancers is enough to say "not obviously worse," not enough to say "validated." I found it only as a 2023 conference abstract and press release; the 2025 peer-reviewed reader study does not break results out by skin tone. The FDA seems to agree the data are thin: its order requires a post-market study that includes "Fitzpatrick skin phototypes IV, V, and VI." And the acral sites where melanoma hides on darker skin are the lesions a light-scattering wand has seen least.

## What I'd watch

- A CPT category III code or a Medicare contractor coverage decision. Either changes the economics overnight.
- Results of the FDA-required study in Fitzpatrick IV to VI, and an independent replication on acral lesions.
- Referral data from a deploying health system. Longer dermatology waits with no earlier melanoma stage would mean the device adds noise, not signal.

## From the clinic

In the Mohs clinic where I work as a medical assistant, nearly every patient arrives already biopsied by a dermatologist. DermaSensor's bet is upstream, in the family doctor's office. It is the right bet. Whether the office can afford to make it is the open question.

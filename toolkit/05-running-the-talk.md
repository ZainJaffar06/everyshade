# 05. Running the talk

The talk is 45 minutes including questions, and it is the same talk every time. You do not need to be a gifted speaker. You need to know the deck, speak slowly, keep the lights on, and follow the Q&A rules.

## The kit

Everything is in talk-kit/ in the repo. Do not edit the slides. If you think a slide is wrong, email everyshadescreening@gmail.com with the slide number and the source you think it should cite; it goes through the review process in section 07.

- **Slides** (PDF and editable deck), about 30 slides. The only slides you change are the title slide (chapter name, your names) and the local resources slide (your area's free and low-cost clinics).
- **Speaker notes**, one paragraph per slide. New presenters read them aloud at first.
- **Large-print handout**, two sides, 16-point minimum. Side one: what to look for, including the acral sites. Side two: the monthly skin check and where to get care. Print one per attendee plus five. Translations are in languages/ (section 08).
- **Pre/post quiz**, ten questions, paper and Zoom-poll versions (section 06).
- **Attendance log**, one sheet per talk.
- **Disclaimer slide and tabletop sign.** The disclaimer is the second and the last slide.

## 45-minute run-of-show

| Time | Segment | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Before | Set up | Arrive 30 minutes early in person, 15 for Zoom. Test sound from the back of the room. Quizzes and handouts on chairs or at the door. |
| 0:00 to 0:03 | Welcome | Names, school, "we're students, not doctors," disclaimer slide read aloud. |
| 0:03 to 0:07 | Pre-quiz | Read each question aloud; launch polls for Zoom. Collect sheets before continuing. |
| 0:07 to 0:12 | Why this talk | The three facts from section 01. Slow down here. |
| 0:12 to 0:22 | What skin cancer looks like on every skin tone | Melanoma, basal cell, squamous cell, side by side across tones. ABCDEs and the ugly duckling. Acral sites and nails get their own slides. |
| 0:22 to 0:30 | The monthly skin check | Step by step from the handout. Full-length mirror, hand mirror, a partner for the back, a phone photo to compare next month. Have people find the spaces between the toes on the diagram. |
| 0:30 to 0:34 | Where to go | Free AAD checks, your local clinics, what to say when you call ("I have a spot that is new and changing and I'd like it looked at"), what to expect at the visit. |
| 0:34 to 0:38 | Post-quiz | Same ten questions. Collect sheets. |
| 0:38 to 0:45 | Questions | Rules below. End on time; stay after for individual conversations within the rules. |

If you run long, cut from the "why" section, never from the skin check or "where to go." If asked for 30 minutes, shorten Q&A and the "why," but never drop the quiz.

## Zoom setup for seniors

Ask which format the organization wants when you book.

**Watch together.** Members gather in one room and watch you on a TV or projector. This is the most common senior center format and the easiest. You need one person in the room with a laptop connected by HDMI, the Zoom link, and your cell number; ask them to join 10 minutes early. Share your screen with presenter view off. Keep your camera on as a small thumbnail so they see a face. Use a headset microphone. Ask the room host to repeat questions into the laptop mic, because you will not hear the back of the room.

**Join from home.** Each member joins from their own device. Send the link and the handout PDF to the coordinator four days ahead and ask for a reminder the morning of. Enable the waiting room, mute on entry, and load the quiz polls in advance. A second chapter member co-hosts: admits latecomers, runs polls, watches the chat, and helps people who cannot connect by phone. Expect a quarter of registrants to have trouble joining.

Both formats: captions on, slides at 100%, no animations or videos. Record only with the organization's written agreement, and never record "join from home" talks.

## Accessibility

Your audience is mostly over 65. Many have low vision, hearing loss, or both, and some have limited English.

- **Large print.** Handout 16-point minimum; slide body text 28-point minimum; never more than four lines on a slide.
- **Speaking pace.** About 120 words per minute. Pause after each slide. Repeat every question before answering.
- **Captions on**, for every Zoom talk. Ask the room host to turn on TV captions for watch-together talks.
- **Plain language.** "Skin cancer," not "cutaneous malignancy." "Spot," not "lesion." "Under the nail," not "subungual." The only medical words in the talk are melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma, each defined in one sentence.
- **No acronyms.** Say "the American Academy of Dermatology," not "the AAD." ABCDE is the one exception, and you explain each letter.
- **Lights on.** People need to see the handout and each other.
- **In person, stand still** and face the room so people can read your lips. Do not talk to the screen.
- **Interpreters.** Section 08. Book one whenever the coordinator says more than a few members prefer another language.

## Q&A rules

1. **Never diagnose a spot.** Not in the room, not after, not from a photo, not by email, not "just as a friend." One wrong "that looks fine" can cost someone a year.
2. **Redirect "Can you look at this?" kindly and the same way every time.** Script below.
3. **Answer general questions from the deck.** If the answer is not on a slide or in the notes: "I don't know, and I don't want to guess. The handout has the Academy's page on that, and your doctor can answer it."
4. **No product advice.** No sunscreen brands, creams, or supplements.
5. **No comments on someone's doctor or care.** If someone says they were dismissed: "You're allowed to ask for a second opinion and to ask specifically for a dermatologist. Here's how to find a free check."
6. **Keep personal stories short.** Thank the person and move on in one sentence.
7. **End on time.** "We have time for two more" at 0:42.

## The redirect script

Someone rolls up a sleeve or holds a foot to the camera: "Can you look at this one?" You say, warmly:

"I appreciate you asking, and I want to be straight with you: I'm a student, not a doctor, so I can't tell you what that is, and I don't want to guess and be wrong. What I can do is help you get it looked at by someone who can. Where would you normally go for care?"

Then one of three paths:

- They have a doctor: "Call this week and say exactly: 'I have a spot that's new and changing and I'd like it looked at.' Those words get you an appointment."
- No doctor or no insurance: "The back of the handout lists places near here that see people for free or on a sliding scale. The first one is [name]. I'll circle it for you."
- They want a dermatologist: "The American Academy of Dermatology runs free skin checks. The website is on the handout. If there isn't one scheduled near you, you can sign up to be told when there is."

Then: "Would you like me to write that down?" Do it, and move to the next question. Never add "but it's probably nothing" or "that does look a little concerning." You do not know, and both can do harm.

If they press: "I know it's frustrating to hear 'see a doctor' when you're asking me. I'd be doing you a disservice if I pretended to know. Let's make sure you can get in to see someone." Then stop.

## If someone describes an urgent symptom

Occasionally someone describes something that should not wait: bleeding that will not stop, a sore open for weeks, a nail band that has spread into the skin, a fast-growing lump, or something unrelated to skin (chest pain, trouble breathing, confusion) that comes up because you are the health person in the room.

- Anything that sounds like an emergency right now (trouble breathing, chest pain, sudden weakness, severe bleeding): stop the talk, tell the room host, and call 911 or have staff do so. You are not diagnosing; you are responding to what the person said. Stay with them until staff take over.
- A skin symptom that needs care soon but is not an emergency: "What you're describing is the kind of thing doctors want to see soon, this week if you can. I can't tell you what it is, but I don't want you to wait." Give the resource list and, with their agreement, give their name to the site coordinator so staff can help them make the call. Do not take their phone number yourself.
- Write what happened (no names) on the attendance log under "incidents" and tell the president the same day. Include it in the semester report.

You will do this rarely. Practice it anyway.

## After the talk

Within 48 hours: quiz sheets entered, attendance log filed, thank-you email to the coordinator with the handout PDF and a request for a repeat date, and anything that went wrong noted for the weekly meeting. At 30 days: the follow-up in section 06.

# 07. Content standards and medical review

The talk is only as safe as its weakest claim. These standards exist so that any slide, handout line, or translated sentence can be traced to a source a physician would accept, and so that a mistake found by one chapter gets fixed for all of them.

## Every claim is traceable

Every factual statement on a slide or handout traces to one of four kinds of sources:

1. The American Academy of Dermatology (aad.org), its public pages or Basic Dermatology Curriculum.
2. The Skin Cancer Foundation (skincancer.org).
3. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (cdc.gov), including Preventing Chronic Disease and U.S. Cancer Statistics.
4. A peer-reviewed paper, cited with authors, journal, year, and DOI.

The speaker notes list the source for each slide. If you want to add a sentence and cannot point to one of these four, you do not add it. "My professor said" is not a source. A news article about a study is not a source; the study is.

The three headline facts and their sources are in section 01. The appearance descriptions come from the AAD page "Finding skin cancer in darker skin tones" (https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/darker-skin-tones). The self-check steps come from the AAD's "How to check your skin" (https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/find/check-skin). The ABCDEs come from https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/find/at-risk/abcdes.

## What the talk does not say

- That sunscreen prevents acral melanoma. It does not; acral melanoma is not driven by sun exposure. The talk recommends sun protection for the sun-related cancers and says plainly that palms, soles, and nails need checking regardless.
- Individual risk numbers ("your risk is X%").
- Product, brand, or clinic recommendations. The local resource list is a list of places that offer free or sliding-scale care, stated as fact, not an endorsement.
- Treatment, beyond "the doctor may take a small sample to test" and "found early, most skin cancers are very treatable."
- Anything about skin lightening, cosmetics, or hair products, except to redirect a question.
- The words "screening" or "exam" for what we do. We give talks. Dermatologists screen.

## Annual physician review

Each September, the speaker lead sends the advisor the current deck, the handout, and the list of "questions we could not answer from the deck" collected in the semester reports. The ask: mark anything wrong, outdated, or overstated, and reply "approved as is" or "approved with these changes." Save the dated reply in the Admin folder and put the date in the semester report.

The Rutgers chapter does the same, and its approved deck is the master. If your advisor requests a change, send it to everyshadescreening@gmail.com with the reasoning and source; we fold it into the master so every chapter benefits and the decks stay identical. Do not maintain a diverging local version. If your advisor insists on a change we decline, talk to us before presenting it. The only slides a chapter changes alone are the title slide and the local resources slide.

If a source changes (a statistic updated, a page moved), email us the new link. We re-verify and push a new kit version. Check the kit's version number against the repo before each semester.

## Never diagnose, in writing either

If someone emails the chapter a photo of a spot, reply with the redirect script, do not comment on the photo, delete it, and do not forward it to anyone, including your advisor. The chapter email is not a clinical channel, and a photo of someone's skin is personal health information you have no business storing.

## Disclaimer wording

Use this exact wording on the footer of any web page, the second and last slide, the handout, the flyer, the chapter email signature, and any social post that describes what we do in more than a sentence:

> Every Shade provides general health education only. Nothing on this site or in our talks is medical advice or a diagnosis. Our members are students, not medical professionals. If a spot is new, changing, or bleeding, please see a doctor.

Change "this site" to "this handout" or "this flyer" as appropriate. Do not shorten it, reword it, or set it smaller than the surrounding text.

## Images

The images are the most sensitive part of the kit. They make the talk work for an audience with brown and Black skin, and they are the easiest place to break copyright or mislead.

**Rules.**

1. Never scrape images. No Google Images, no textbook screenshots, no figures copied from journal articles, no social media, and no photos of patients from any clinic you work at, ever, even with the face cropped. "For education" does not make an image yours to use.
2. Use only images whose license permits non-commercial educational use, with the source written in the slide notes.
3. Show each condition on at least two skin tones side by side wherever the source allows. A melanoma slide that shows only light skin is worse than no slide.
4. Attribute every image on its slide or on a sources slide, as the license requires.
5. Images stay in the deck. Chapters do not post deck images to social media or print them on flyers.

**Sources we use** (verified November 2026; re-check the terms before reusing anything):

- **The Full Spectrum of Dermatology: A Diverse and Inclusive Dermatology Atlas**, from the Journal of Drugs in Dermatology. Its free online gallery at https://jddonline.com/project-atlas/ offers side-by-side images across skin tones and states that they may be used free of charge for non-commercial educational purposes, subject to the site's Image Use Policy. Read that policy and attribute as it requires. The print and digital editions of the atlas are paid products.
- **The AAD's public skin cancer pages**, including "Finding skin cancer in darker skin tones" and the per-cancer image galleries. These are for reading and linking. The images are copyrighted; link to the page from the handout rather than copying images unless you have written permission from the AAD.
- **The Skin Cancer Foundation's "Skin Cancer and Skin of Color" brochure** (PDF on skincancer.org). Hand it out as-is and link to it. Do not extract its images.
- **Vaseline's See My Skin**, launched in 2022 with VisualDx and HUED as a searchable set of skin conditions on skin of color, is often recommended. As of November 2026, seemyskin.com redirects to the HUED provider directory (directory.huedco.com), which no longer offers the condition image search. Do not plan on it as an image source. VisualDx itself is a paid clinical tool; do not screenshot it.

To add an image from any other source, send the image, the source URL, and the license text to everyshadescreening@gmail.com. If it clears, it goes into the master deck.

## Translation quality

Every translated handout in languages/ must be reviewed by a native speaker with a medical or health background (physician, nurse, pharmacist, certified medical interpreter, or a medical, nursing, pharmacy, or public health student who grew up speaking the language) before any chapter gives it out. The reviewer checks that the medical meaning is preserved (especially "melanoma," "under the nail," "new or changing," and the disclaimer), that the reading level is plain, and that the terms are the ones people in that community use. The reviewer's name, credential, and date go in the file header. A machine translation that has not been reviewed is a draft, marked DRAFT in the file name, and is never given to an audience. Section 08 explains how to find reviewers.

## If you find an error

Email everyshadescreening@gmail.com the same day with the slide or handout line, what you think is wrong, and the source. If it could lead someone to delay care, skip that slide until you hear back. We fix the master, bump the version, and tell every chapter. Nobody gets in trouble for finding an error. Chapters get in trouble for presenting one they knew about.

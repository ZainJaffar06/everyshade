# 06. Measuring outcomes

You measure three things: did people learn (pre/post quiz), how many people (attendance log), and did they do a skin check afterward (30-day follow-up). Everything is anonymous. The point is not to prove the program works. The point is to find out whether it does, and to fix the parts that do not.

## The pre/post quiz protocol

Ten questions, the same ten before and after, in talk-kit/ as a paper sheet and a Zoom poll file. Do not change the questions; send suggestions to Every Shade (Rutgers) so every chapter changes at once and the numbers stay comparable.

The ten questions cover: whether people with dark skin can get skin cancer; where melanoma most often appears in Black patients; what a nail band can mean; the ABCDEs; what basal cell carcinoma can look like on dark skin; how often to check your skin; what to do about a spot that is new or changing; whether sunscreen prevents melanoma on the palms and soles; where to get a free check; and whether the presenters can diagnose a spot. The last one checks that "we are students, not doctors" landed.

**In person (paper).** Hand out the pre-quiz as people sit down. Read every question aloud, slowly, 20 seconds each. Collect the sheets before the "why" section. At minute 34, hand out the post-quiz on a different-colored sheet, read it the same way, collect it. Pencils on every chair. Large print, one question per line.

**Virtual (Zoom polls).** The co-host creates polls "Pre 1" to "Pre 10" and "Post 1" to "Post 10" from the kit file in advance. Launch each pre poll for 30 seconds while reading the question aloud, close it, move on. Do not share results on screen during the pre-quiz. Repeat with the post polls at minute 34. Afterward, download the poll report from Zoom.

**Watch together.** The room is in person, so use paper. The room host scans or mails the sheets. Arrange this when you book.

**Anonymous.** No names, initials, or birthdays. The only optional extras are an age range and whether the person has ever had a skin check. Collected this way, it is program evaluation, not human subjects research; if you ever want to publish, talk to your advisor about IRB first, and ask us, because the Rutgers chapter has been through it.

## Computing knowledge gain

Enter each talk in the data sheet in talk-kit/: one tab per talk, one row per sheet, one column per question, 1 for correct and 0 for wrong or blank.

**Per question:** percent correct before = correct pre answers divided by pre sheets. Percent correct after = correct post answers divided by post sheets. Gain = after minus before, in percentage points.

**Overall:** mean score before = average of the ten pre percentages; mean score after likewise; overall gain = the difference.

Example, 22 pre sheets and 20 post sheets: question 2 (where melanoma most often appears in Black patients) went from 5 of 22 (23%) to 18 of 20 (90%), a 67-point gain. Question 6 (how often to check) went from 15 of 22 (68%) to 17 of 20 (85%), a 17-point gain. Overall mean: 54% to 86%.

Because sheets are anonymous, you are comparing the group before with the group after, not each person with themselves. That is fine for program evaluation; do not describe it as "each person improved by X." Report the number of sheets, not the number in the room. Under eight post sheets, report the numbers and say the group was small.

## Attendance log

One line per talk: date, organization, town, type (senior center / library / faith / community / other), format (in person / watch together / join from home), headcount (count the room at minute 10, or the Zoom participant count), pre sheets, post sheets, presenters, interpreter and language, handout languages given out, incidents (yes/no, with a no-names note), and whether the organization asked for a repeat. Headcount is a count, not an estimate.

## 30-day follow-up

The coordinator has the members' contact information; you do not. When you book, ask: "Thirty days after the talk, would you forward a two-question message to the people who attended? It helps us know whether the talk changes anything." Send them this text at day 28 and ask them to forward it at day 30 by email, text, or paper at the front desk.

```
Hello from Every Shade at [School]. About a month ago you came to our
talk on skin cancer and the monthly skin check. Two quick questions,
no name needed:

1. Since the talk, have you done a skin check at home?
   (Yes / No / Not yet but I plan to)
2. Since the talk, have you made an appointment or gone to get a spot
   looked at? (Yes / No / I didn't need to)

Reply to this message or drop the paper at the front desk. Thank you,
and please keep checking once a month.
```

Report replies as "of N attendees, R replied; of those, X said they had done a skin check." Low reply rates are normal. Do not report only the yeses. Do not collect names or numbers. If the coordinator prefers to ask in person and tell you the counts, note "counts reported by coordinator."

## Reporting numbers honestly

Say exactly what you measured, with the n, and nothing more.

- Good: "Knowledge improved from 54% to 86% correct (n = 22 before, 20 after), a 32-point gain."
- Good: "On where melanoma appears in Black patients, 23% answered correctly before and 90% after."
- Good: "Of 31 attendees, 9 replied to the 30-day follow-up and 6 said they had done a skin check."
- Bad: "Our talk improved knowledge by 60%." (Sixty percent of what?)
- Bad: "Attendees were 4x more likely to do a skin check." (You have no comparison group.)
- Bad: "We've reached hundreds of seniors." (Say the number.)
- Bad: "Our program saves lives." (You have no evidence of that.)

Never drop a talk because the gain was small. A 4-point gain usually means the pre-quiz was already high (a repeat audience) or the room could not hear. Report it and look at why.
